## Article 2 — Product-demo quality checks

**Proposed title:** SaaS product demo QA: check the interface, captions and sequence before publishing

**Slug:** saas-product-demo-quality-checklist

**Primary query hypothesis:** SaaS product demo quality checklist

**Related questions:** Is the UI readable? Do captions cover controls? Does the video show the correct steps? What should reviewers flag?

**Reader and trigger:** A marketer or editor has a finished demo and needs a focused final review. **Intent/page type:** Practical QA checklist. **Funnel role:** Help an existing evaluation/production task, rather than persuade the reader to switch tools.

**Core answer:** Review the exported video against the actual product and intended viewing context, then record specific corrections. A polished animation does not establish that the product explanation is accurate.

**Suggested sections:**

1. Use the current product interface as the reference. Check labels, controls, state changes and the demonstrated sequence. Do not suggest generating an imitation interface as the accuracy reference.
2. Check readability in the actual output. Inspect crops, zooms, text, caption placement and scenes that pass too quickly. Do not invent universal font sizes or hold durations.
3. Check narration, captions and transitions against the action shown. Preserve sentence meaning and product terminology.
4. Make reviewer feedback actionable. Use a table: timestamp → issue → expected correction → owner → recheck status. Show one explicitly illustrative row.
5. Recheck the corrected export and approve the relevant version.

**Distinct contribution:** Concrete inspection and correction logging. Article 1 decides whether to evaluate a workflow; this article reviews the video already produced. Do not spend half the article on tool selection.

**Usable evidence:** Generalized production stages and finishing effort support the topic's context. The actual UI/caption tests are proposed editorial checks, not problems confirmed across the research sample. The source bank's scene-editing and subtitle documentation can support narrowly attributed mechanics; neither guarantees demo accuracy.

**Example:** A caption overlaps the control the narration tells the viewer to select. Label this illustrative; describe moving the caption and rechecking the exported scene. No product-specific menu instructions without a verified source.

**Sources:** S4, S5; use S1 only if a relevant, attributed capability example is useful. **Next action:** Review one export and create a timestamped correction log. **Exclude:** Brand rankings, conversion/engagement claims, guaranteed UI fidelity, privacy/security guarantees and fictional respondent results.

## Article 3 — Editable handoffs for generated assets

**Proposed title:** AI-generated clips to an editable SaaS video: plan the handoff

**Slug:** ai-generated-video-editable-handoff

**Primary query hypothesis:** AI-generated video editable handoff

**Related questions:** Is a finished clip the same as an editable project? Which source files should I retain? What can disappear during timeline export?

**Reader and trigger:** A maker has generated assets and is preparing to finish or hand the video to another editor. **Intent/page type:** Handoff planning guide. **Funnel role:** Help define usable output and a practical evaluation requirement.

**Core answer:** Plan the files and elements that need later correction before generation or export. An MP4 is a finished media file; it does not by itself demonstrate that the original captions, layers and animation settings remain separately editable.

**Suggested sections:**

1. List what the next editor needs to change: interface footage, captions, narration, scene duration and motion timing.
2. Keep source assets and project materials separate from the final export. Propose a simple handoff inventory: asset, source/location, revision, edit needed, responsible person. Include usage/licensing notes where relevant; do not claim that generated output is automatically cleared for any commercial use.
3. Distinguish rendered media, native project sharing and timeline interchange. Explain the difference plainly; do not claim all editors support all three.
4. Verify what survives transfer. Use S6's documented limitations as one attributed example, not a universal rule or evidence that a competing tool is better.
5. Run one correction after reopening the handoff in the receiving editor. If access is unavailable, mark it unverified instead of calling the handoff successful.

**Distinct contribution:** Source retention and transfer checks. This narrows the older editor-versus-generator candidate; article 1 already handles the broad choice. Keep general AI category definitions brief and do not write another best-tools list.

**Usable evidence:** Noneditable generated-output context and manual finishing accounts motivate the checks. They do not establish measured handoff failure, a market-wide problem or Cardboard's superiority.

**Example:** An illustrative teammate needs to replace a product screen and adjust the narration. Show which source assets and editing materials they would need. Do not imply a native project or XML preserves every effect.

**Sources:** S4, S6 and, optionally, S1. **Next action:** Build a handoff inventory and verify one correction in the receiving workflow. **Exclude:** Guaranteed round-trip fidelity, unsupported format claims, measured savings and unverified copyright assurances.
